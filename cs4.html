Designing the research for a tool that doesn't exist yet

Discovery research for VORTEX, a next-generation decision support tool for aviation professionals. Research that shaped what the product should be, before a single line of code was written.

Senior UX Researcher (Lead)
Discovery
Contextual inquiry, co-design
Research strategy and direction

The Challenge

Building something new in a safety-critical environment

VORTEX is a decision support product in early discovery: a tool intended to help aviation professionals make faster, safer decisions by synthesising multiple weather data sources into a single, actionable interface. The challenge was that no equivalent product existed for reference. There was no established user mental model to test against.

My role was to design and lead the discovery research: establishing what aviation professionals actually needed from a decision support tool, how they currently made decisions, and what risks a poorly designed product could introduce into their workflow.

The constraint: In safety-critical research, the cost of designing toward the wrong mental model is not a bad product. It is a product that undermines operational safety. The research had to be thorough, not fast.

The Approach

Understanding decisions before designing for them

Contextual inquiry Cognitive task analysis Co-design workshops Expert interviews Scenario-based research Mental model mapping

Rather than beginning with features or interfaces, I designed research around decision points: the specific moments in a pilot's or meteorologist's workflow where weather information is consulted, synthesised, and acted upon. Cognitive task analysis let me surface not just what users do, but the reasoning and uncertainty behind each step.

Co-design workshops with subject matter experts gave the team a shared vocabulary for what decision support could mean in practice, and surfaced constraints from the domain that no amount of interface testing would have revealed.

What I Found

Decisions are not moments. They are processes.

Insight 01

Aviation decisions involve layered uncertainty, not binary choices

Pilots and meteorologists don't make a single go/no-go decision. They continuously update their assessment as new information arrives. A decision support tool that presents a single recommendation misrepresents how decisions are actually made.

Insight 02

Trust in automated tools is earned incrementally, not assumed

Experienced aviators were sceptical of tools that consolidated information without showing their working. Transparency of data sources and reasoning was not a nice-to-have: it was the condition for adoption.

Insight 03

The most dangerous moment is handoff between information sources

When users switch between data sources, they carry incomplete mental models from each. A decision support tool that doesn't account for this transition creates exactly the kind of cognitive load it should be reducing.

What I'd do differently

I would build a more formal relationship with domain experts from the start: meteorologists and experienced pilots as research partners, not just participants. In safety-critical discovery, the subject matter expertise is as important as the research methodology.
