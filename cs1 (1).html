The Challenge

A newly digitised service with invisible problems

The Approved Premises service exists to prevent homelessness and reoffending. When someone is due to leave prison, a probation practitioner completes a referral to secure them a place in supported accommodation. Before this project, that referral happened via a PDF form: slow, error-prone, and deeply frustrating for the practitioners who depended on it daily.

The newly digitised service had launched in private beta across two regions with over 3,000 users. Early signals pointed to usability problems affecting how reliably applications were completing. The team needed to understand exactly what was going wrong before committing to national rollout.

Service architecture: the Approved Premises digital service journey, from referral trigger to placement

The Approach

Observation over assumption

Semi-structured interviews Usability testing Contextual inquiry Observational site visit Card sorting

I recruited 6 probation practitioners who had each completed at least one live application: not prototype users, but people with real operational experience. I chose contextual inquiry specifically because the application involved multiple systems, and friction between them was invisible until you watched someone navigate between them under time pressure.

I chose an observational site visit over remote research because the application process could not be fully understood from a screen-share. Visiting a real Approved Premises meant I could see the physical environment, the off-hours risk created by printing failures, and the way allocation decisions were made in practice, not just in theory.

What I Found

Three problems breaking the service

"No OASys information took around 40 minutes. My colleagues would take an hour or hour and a half."

Probation Practitioner, private beta participant

Three issues were generating the most friction. The assessment system was failing to pull through correctly, adding up to 40 minutes per application. Practitioners had no visibility of application progress after submission, creating anxiety and unnecessary follow-up calls. And observational research revealed something no interview had: completing an application fully took 2 hours and 20 minutes, driven by the volume and format of assessment data being imported.

I also identified that the service was built around "beds" while staff needed to think in "spaces": a distinction that only became clear through live observation of how allocation decisions were actually made on the ground.

Insight 01

The failure was invisible until you watched it happen

A PDF system issue adding 40 minutes per application had never been formally raised, because users had normalised it. Live observation, not interviews, surfaced it.

Insight 02

The mental model mismatch ran deeper than the interface

The service was built around individual beds. Staff allocate by available space, factoring in resident needs and room suitability. Two different logics, built into the same product.

Insight 03

Communication silence was breaking trust

Practitioners submitted applications and heard nothing. The anxiety this created was generating phone calls, workarounds, and reduced confidence in the entire service.

Opportunities & Recommendations

From insight to design direction

Each finding pointed to a concrete opportunity. Below are the recommendations I brought to the team, grounded in what assessors and practitioners told me and what I observed directly.

Recommendation 01 · Application form

Make critical assessment fields mandatory at submission

The move to an online form had not resolved the underlying data problem. Assessors were still receiving applications missing key information such as behavioural concerns, accessibility needs and prison leave dates, and were spending significant time chasing practitioners to fill the gaps. This created delays at every stage: assessment, placement, and ultimately, people leaving prison on time.

My recommendation was to work with assessors to identify the minimum set of information required to review an application, then surface those fields as mandatory in the probation officer submission form. Incomplete applications would be blocked from submission. This removed the back-and-forth entirely and accelerated the assessment process.

Recommendation 02 · Room allocation

Rename beds as spaces and give officers manual allocation control

Assessors explained that room suitability was never just about availability. It depended on the resident's specific needs. A room without adequate ventilation, for example, was inappropriate for someone with anger-management issues; an enclosed room could be unsuitable for someone with claustrophobia. The system's automated logic didn't account for any of this.

My recommendation was twofold: rename "beds" as "spaces" to reflect how staff actually think about allocation, and give resident officers the ability to assign any resident to any room manually. Removing the automated process restored the human judgement that allocation decisions require.

Recommendation 03 · Communication

Introduce a status tracking system visible to practitioners

After submitting an application, practitioners received no updates. The silence generated anxiety, unnecessary phone calls chasing progress, and informal workarounds, all of which eroded confidence in the service. The problem wasn't capacity; it was the absence of information.

My recommendation was to introduce a status tracking system that surfaced the current phase of each application to the practitioner who submitted it, with clear stage labels and expected timescales. Giving practitioners visibility into their applications' progress would eliminate the information void and remove the trigger for unnecessary contact.

What I'd do differently

Research began after the service was already in beta, meaning structural issues surfaced later than ideal. Given the opportunity again, I would embed ethnographic research from alpha: diary studies and site visits during discovery, not beta, so that operational workflow complexity could shape the service design from the start rather than prompt revisions to it.
