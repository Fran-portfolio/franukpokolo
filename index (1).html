Ministry of Justice  ·  dxw Digital Consultancy  ·  2023

Research that helped people leave prison safely

Evaluative research in private beta for a digital accommodation referral service used by 8,000+ probation practitioners nationwide; helping prevent homelessness and reoffending among people leaving the justice system

My RoleUser Researcher (Lead)
Team9 across product, design, engineering
Beta Users3,000+ across 2 regions
National Scale8,000+ practitioners

The Challenge

The Approved Premises service exists to prevent homelessness and reoffending. When someone is due to leave prison, a probation practitioner completes a referral to secure them a place in supported accommodation. Before this project, that referral happened via a PDF form; slow, error-prone, and deeply frustrating for the practitioners who depended on it daily.

The newly digitised service had launched in private beta across two regions with over 3,000 users. Early signals pointed to usability problems affecting how reliably applications were completing. The team needed to understand exactly what was going wrong before committing to national rollout.

The Approach

Semi-structured interviewsUsability testingContextual inquiryObservational site visitCard sorting

I recruited 6 probation practitioners who had each completed at least one live application; not prototype users, but people with real operational experience. I chose contextual inquiry specifically because the application involved multiple systems, not just the new service, and friction between them was invisible until you watched someone navigate between them under time pressure.

I chose observational site visit over remote research because the application process could not be fully understood from a screen-share. Visiting a real Approved Premises meant I could see the physical environment, the off-hours risk created by printing failures, and the way allocation decisions were made in practice, not just in theory.

What I Found

“No OASys information took around 40 minutes. My colleagues would take an hour or hour and a half.”

Probation Practitioner, private beta participant

Three issues were generating the most friction. The assessment system was failing to pull through correctly, adding up to 40 minutes per application. Practitioners had no visibility of application progress after submission, creating anxiety and unnecessary follow-up calls. And observational research revealed something no interview had: completing an application fully took 2 hours and 20 minutes, driven by the volume and format of assessment data being imported.

I also identified that the service was built around “beds” while staff needed to think in “spaces”; a distinction that only became clear through live observation of how allocation decisions were actually made on the ground.

Key Insights

01

The failure was invisible until you watched it happen

A PDF system issue adding 40 minutes per application had never been formally raised, because users had normalised it. Live observation, not interviews, surfaced it.

02

The mental model mismatch ran deeper than the interface

The service was built around individual beds; staff allocate by available space, factoring in resident needs and room suitability. Two different logics, built into the same product.

03

Communication silence was breaking trust

Practitioners submitted applications and heard nothing. The anxiety this created was generating phone calls, workarounds, and reduced confidence in the entire service.

The Impact

50%increase in successfully submitted applications after recommendations were implemented
11,000successful transactions completed in a single month following improvements
~30 minestimated saving per application if OASys pull-through was resolved
Dec 2023service approved for national rollout to 8,000+ practitioners

Recommendations informed the service communication redesign, in-service guidance, and a rethink of how accommodation allocation worked. The service passed its quality review and progressed to national rollout on schedule.

What I’d Do Differently

Research began after the service was already in beta, meaning structural issues surfaced later than ideal. Given the opportunity again, I would embed ethnographic research from alpha; diary studies and site visits during discovery, not beta, so that operational workflow complexity could shape the service design from the start rather than prompt revisions to it.

Met Office  ·  MAVIS  ·  2025–2026

Seeing the problem before users could describe it

Navigation research, cross-functional synthesis workshops, and GA4 analysis for MAVIS; a unified aviation weather platform serving 500,000+ pilots, meteorologists, and drone operators across the UK and beyond

My RoleSenior UX Researcher (Lead)
Platform Users500,000+ aviators
Public LaunchMarch 2026
MethodsMixed qual and quant

The Challenge

MAVIS is used daily by pilots, glider instructors, drone operators, and operational meteorologists to make go/no-go flight decisions. Getting weather information wrong, or being unable to find it quickly, is not a usability problem. In aviation, it is a safety problem.

During observation of users in public beta, I noticed something not yet appearing in formal feedback: users were consistently taking longer than expected to locate the specific weather layers they needed. No one had complained. But the hesitation patterns were there; small, repeated moments of uncertainty in an environment where there is no margin for uncertainty.

The Approach

Behavioural observationTask-based usability testingCard sortingCross-functional synthesis workshopGA4 analysisProduct telemetry

I chose card sorting over tree testing because the problem was not whether users could follow an existing structure; it was whether that structure made sense in the first place. Task-based usability testing (rather than heuristic review) allowed me to observe real behaviour under realistic conditions, capturing hesitation and backtracking that completion metrics alone would not have shown.

I also designed and ran a cross-functional synthesis workshop with product managers, designers, and developers; not just to share findings, but to move directly from user feedback to defined user needs to prioritised Jira tickets. This compressed weeks of potential back-and-forth into a single structured session.

Research Process

1
Flagged early
Raised navigation concern proactively from observation before it appeared in formal feedback
→
2
Designed study
Task-based usability sessions and card sort targeting navigation and information structure
→
3
Ran sessions
Pilots, meteorologists, drone operators; behavioural and qualitative data captured
→
4
GA4 analysis
Analysed map layer usage data from 20,000+ launch-day users to quantify real behaviour
→
5
Workshopped
Cross-functional session: feedback to user needs to Jira tickets with product, design, engineering
→
6

Prioritised recommendations to Delivery Manager; cloud base business case built and accepted

What I Found

“I know the information is there somewhere; I just don't know where to look first.”

General aviation pilot, MAVIS usability session

Users could complete tasks eventually, but only through hesitation, backtracking, and trial and error. In operational conditions, under time pressure, these patterns become dangerous. The card sorting confirmed that the service structure did not reflect user mental models.

GA4 analysis revealed that Cloud Base; a layer deprioritised in the roadmap; was the most accessed feature among general aviation users with 16,906 events in the first 28 days. Cross-referenced with interview data on flight safety, this made the case for urgent intervention undeniable.

Key Insights

01

Task completion masked the real problem

Users were technically completing tasks but relying on trial and error to do so. Standard completion metrics would have shown an acceptable pass rate; observation revealed a service that was quietly dangerous under time pressure.

02

Quantitative data reversed a roadmap decision

Cloud Base had been deprioritised based on assumed demand. GA4 showed it was the most-used feature. Without that data, a safety-critical fix would have waited months.

03

Involving the team in synthesis accelerated delivery

Running a structured workshop where developers saw user evidence directly changed the tone of prioritisation conversations; work that might have taken three sprints to agree was committed in a single session.

MAVIS Navigation Workshop; card sort results, user route mapping, and frequency/impact prioritisation across three navigation scenarios

Cross-functional synthesis session; product, design, engineering, and research moving from raw feedback to defined needs to Jira tickets in a single session

GA4 exploration (MAVIS, Apr–May 2026); Cloud Base ranked as the most accessed map layer with 16,906 events among general aviation users, despite being deprioritised in the roadmap. This data formed the business case for an urgent safety-critical fix.

The Impact

20,000+users analysed via GA4 on launch day; revealing real usage patterns before formal feedback emerged
2 monthsto deliver a technically complex cloud base fix post-launch; driven by the research-backed business case
3major product changes: full IA review, context-aware information surfacing, and a UX customisation feature

The Delivery Manager introduced weekly epic reviews structured around my prioritisation of high-impact areas. The cloud base fix, technically complex, was delivered within two months of launch and directly improved safety outcomes for thousands of pilots.

What I’d Do Differently

I would narrow the initial study scope earlier and prioritise two or three critical flows rather than the full surface area at once. The breadth generated genuinely useful data, but a tighter first pass focused on the highest-risk navigation moments would have initiated design iteration faster.

Met Office  ·  VORTEX  ·  2024–2025

The research that changed what we built

Alpha discovery and end-to-end workflow research on VORTEX; a next-generation platform for creating and distributing 280+ regulated meteorological data products underpinning aviation safety and emergency response systems

My RoleUX Researcher, promoted to Senior
TimelineNov 2024 to Jun 2025 (8 months)
Scope280+ regulated data products
UsersMeteorologists, DevOps Engineers

The Challenge

VORTEX was a next-generation platform to modernise how the Met Office creates, manages, and distributes over 280 regulated meteorological data products; replacing legacy systems relied upon by aviation authorities, emergency services, and government agencies. I joined the team during alpha, with a planned service release on a defined trajectory.

My role was to evaluate the service. What I found required something more than evaluation.

The Approach

End-to-end usability testingScenario-based workflow analysisIn-depth interviewsExperimental A/B variant testingResearchOps build

I designed the programme around end-to-end workflow testing rather than feature-level evaluation; deliberately. The system supported a continuous operational process, and testing individual screens in isolation would have made the experience look far better than it was. I needed to see how friction accumulated across the whole journey: template creation, workflow configuration, and product editing together, not separately.

I also built the team's ResearchOps infrastructure from scratch; templates, ethics frameworks, recruitment processes, and a research repository. The team had not had a researcher before, and I needed to create the conditions for consistent, traceable research to happen across the project, not just in this phase.

Research Process

1
Built ops
Established ResearchOps infrastructure: templates, ethics, recruitment, repository from zero
→
2
Planned
Designed end-to-end scenario test plan covering template creation, configuration, and product editing
→
3
Ran sessions
Meteorologists and DevOps engineers completing realistic operational tasks across the full workflow
→
4
Experimented
A/B variant testing on terminology and workflow presentation to validate specific design changes
→
5

Identified cumulative friction across the journey and the OPMET structural mismatch
→
6

Presented findings to senior stakeholders; OPMET launch reprioritised; 70%+ buy-in secured

What I Found

“It's not one thing; it's just that every step takes a bit longer than it should.”

Operational meteorologist, VORTEX usability session

Within five months I identified critical misalignments between the planned OPMET service release and what users actually needed. The problems were structural. Proceeding would have deployed a service that forced meteorologists to work against their operational logic, in a system responsible for regulated products that aviation safety infrastructure depends on.

Beyond OPMET, the end-to-end workflow testing revealed something isolated feature tests would have missed: small, repeated points of friction were compounding across the journey, adding over 60 minutes to tasks that needed to be completed accurately and under time pressure.

Key Insights

01

End-to-end testing revealed what screen-by-screen testing hides

Each individual screen appeared functional in isolation. Watching the whole workflow revealed cumulative cognitive load that no feature-level evaluation would have caught; the system was technically working but operationally draining.

02

Structural misalignment is harder to catch than surface friction

The OPMET issue was not a confusing button or unclear label. It was a fundamental mismatch between the system's logic and the mental model of the people who had to use it under time pressure. Only whole-workflow observation surfaces this.

03

Research built in alpha prevents reconstruction in beta

The 2-month delay to the OPMET launch was not a setback; it was the research working exactly as it should. Acting on evidence in alpha costs weeks. Rebuilding after a flawed launch costs months.

Collaborative problem/solution space workshop; aligning product, design, and engineering on user problems and insights before exploring solutions, with impact/effort prioritisation to focus delivery

The Impact

2 monthsstrategic reprioritisation of OPMET launch; preventing a costly and high-risk misalignment
60+ minsaved per task for meteorologists and DevOps engineers through workflow improvements
70%+stakeholder buy-in for research-backed decisions in a team new to user-centred practice
8 monthsfrom joining as UX Researcher to promotion to Senior; a direct result of this project's impact

The research changed what was built, when it was built, and how the team made decisions. That is the outcome research should produce.

What I’d Do Differently

I would bring product and design stakeholders into sessions earlier; not just at playback, but as observers during live research. The moment a stakeholder watches a user struggle in real time, the conversation about prioritisation changes in a way that even the clearest written debrief cannot replicate.

Met Office  ·  MAVIS and RITO  ·  2025–2026

Making research impossible to ignore

Designing and building a research-to-delivery pipeline for a 500,000-user aviation platform; and founding Research in the Open, an org-wide ResearchOps initiative now independently adopted across the Met Office

My RoleSenior UX Researcher (ResearchOps Lead)
Tools BuiltPower Automate, SharePoint, Jira PD
ReachOrg-wide adoption beyond original team
InitiativeResearch in the Open (RITO)

The Challenge

The research existed. The problem was that it was not consistently shaping decisions. Feedback from sessions, surveys, and user communications sat in fragments; emails in one place, session notes in another, different tools for different teams. Prioritisation often came down to whoever made the strongest argument in a meeting, rather than what the cumulative evidence said.

Separately, there was no shared knowledge system across the UX community at the Met Office. Research findings sat in individual project folders, invisible to teams working on related problems. Good work was being repeated. Hard-won insight was being lost.

The Approach

Systems designPower Automate pipelineSharePoint data architectureJira Product Discovery integrationUCD discovery with 15 practitionersJourney mappingHow Might We workshops

For the pipeline, I used Power Automate to capture feedback from multiple sources into a structured SharePoint dataset. The critical design decision was integrating this directly into Jira Product Discovery; not a separate repository requiring effort to consult, but the tool the team already used to prioritise work, so evidence was present at the moment decisions were made.

For RITO, I treated the initiative itself as a research project: discovery interviews with 15 UCD professionals, journey mapping of the research-to-decision flow, and How Might We workshops to generate solutions. The knowledge system that resulted was built on what practitioners actually needed, not what I assumed they needed.

What I Found

“We hear a lot from users, but it's hard to know what actually matters most.”

Product Manager, MAVIS team

Once the pipeline was live, patterns emerged that fragmentation had hidden. Specific user needs were being raised repeatedly across multiple sources and contexts but had never been connected. Their true frequency had not registered because no one could see the full picture. The system made these patterns visible for the first time.

The shift was from reactive, discussion-led prioritisation to structured, evidence-led decision-making. Conversations became faster and more focused because the evidence was already organised before the meeting began.

Key Insights

01

More data does not equal better decisions; structure does

Early versions of the pipeline captured everything and created overwhelm. The insight was that teams needed frequency and pattern visibility, not volume. Linking feedback to defined user needs, not just tagging it, was what made the system usable.

02

Research tools only work if they live where decisions happen

Putting insights into a SharePoint folder that teams had to remember to visit changed nothing. Integrating into Jira; the tool already open in every sprint planning session; made the evidence unavoidable.

03

The RITO knowledge system outlasted its origin project

Teams not involved in RITO's design chose to adopt it independently. That adoption is the strongest evidence of genuine usefulness; it means the system solved a real problem, not just a local one.

RITO discovery; affinity mapping from interviews with 15 UCD professionals, exploring how research was currently managed and where insight was being lost. This discovery phase informed the design of the knowledge system now adopted org-wide.

The MAVIS User Needs database; structured user needs captured via Power Automate, each linked to evidence, user groups, frequency scores, and Jira Product Discovery. Before this system, these patterns were invisible; after it, they became the starting point for every prioritisation conversation.

The Impact

3+feedback sources integrated into one structured system: research sessions, surveys, and direct communications
Org-wideRITO adoption chosen independently by teams not involved in its design
Every sprintproduct managers referencing structured user evidence in prioritisation as standard practice
15UCD professionals involved in RITO discovery; making the initiative itself a model of what it was embedding

The system demonstrably changed how teams worked. Independent adoption beyond the original team was the strongest signal that it solved a real problem.

What I’d Do Differently

I would define success metrics for both systems at the outset; adoption rates, reduction in time spent on prioritisation discussions, proportion of backlog decisions traceable to structured evidence. The systems changed how teams worked, but without baseline measurements taken before launch, quantifying that change precisely was harder than it should have been.

Met Office  ·  TOM Decision Support  ·  2025–2026

Translating weather intelligence into operational decisions

Prototype testing and discovery research for a decision support service at Heathrow; exploring how the Met Office could shift from forecast delivery to decision-centred services in safety-critical aviation environments

My RoleSenior UX Researcher, Transport Department Lead
Duration16-week Discovery and Alpha
TestbedHeathrow Airport (embedded on-site)
Roles Researched12+ operational roles across APOC and Heathrow

The Challenge

Operational decisions in weather-critical environments are increasingly complex, time-pressured, and high-impact. Yet the research found that customers were struggling to translate weather intelligence into the operational actions they needed to take. There was a clear gap between what the Met Office produced and what its customers needed in order to act on it confidently.

The TOM (Transforming Operational Meteorology) programme commissioned a 16-week Discovery and Alpha to understand how decisions were actually being made at Heathrow Airport, test prototype concepts for a future decision support service, and identify where the biggest gaps and opportunities lay. As Senior UX Researcher and Transport Department Research Lead, I led the prototype testing component and captured gaps between the proposed concepts and real operational workflows.

The Approach

Ethnographic embeddingContextual observationIn-depth interviewsCo-design workshopConcept testing (2 rounds)Storyboard walkthroughsInteractive prototype testingPrioritisation workshop

I chose ethnographic embedding over remote interviews because decision-making at an airport cannot be understood from outside it. Being present in APOC alongside operational meteorologists meant I could observe the informal interactions, the “water cooler moments”, and the workarounds that formal interviews never surface. These unguarded moments revealed as much about the service as structured sessions.

For prototype testing, I used scenario-based sessions with storyboards and interactive dashboard prototypes rather than open exploration; because I needed participants to engage with the concepts in the context of real decisions they would actually face, such as a snow event or sudden wind change, not evaluate them as abstract design objects.

What I Found

“The only thing that makes our role interesting is when there is an event.”

Met Office Operational Meteorologist, Heathrow APOC

The current service was not perceived as broken; where human interaction was concerned, it was valued highly. But a clear gap had opened between what the service provides today and what users expect a modern decision support service to offer. Products were described as static, visually limited, and unchanged for years.

Op Mets were spending the majority of their time producing forecast products through slow, manual processes; leaving limited capacity for the high-value, insight-led engagement that both they and their customers wanted. The “human in the loop” model worked well within APOC but did not extend far enough across the wider operational landscape; teams including Landside Operations and Engineering were using Met Office weather data but had never spoken to an Op Met.

Key Insights

01

Decision-making is better understood through mindsets than job titles

Four mindsets emerged from the research; Strategic Shapers, System Orchestrators, Operational Responders, and Local Planners; each requiring a different form of certainty and a different kind of decision support. Job titles cut across these mindsets in ways that made role-based design misleading.

02

Forecasts lose precision as they travel through the organisation

Forecasts were being amended multiple times as they moved from Met Office output to team-specific formats across Heathrow. By the time a decision was made, the original forecast context had often been lost; reducing trust and increasing the risk of misinterpretation.

03

Preventing disruption in severe weather is where the real value lives

Heathrow were clear that decisions like whether to de-ice or cancel flights; where a wrong call costs millions; are where they want focused decision support. Routine fair-weather services are table stakes; the differentiating value is in high-stakes, time-pressured events.

The Impact

5service pillars defined for a future decision support service; automation, expertise distribution, decision-centred design, contextual communication, and integrated teamworking
4decision-making mindsets identified; providing a user-led framework to prioritise which groups to design for first
3-phaseroadmap produced from Aug 2026 through 2027 and beyond; covering winter readiness, communication improvement, and automation
1Heathrow Advisor role recommended; directly informed by the research on where embedded expertise creates most value

Findings directly informed the go/no-go on MVP scope and provided the evidence base for investment decisions in the programme's next phase. The service pillars framework is now guiding the direction of future Alpha and Beta work.

What I’d Do Differently

Testing concepts at the right level of fidelity is difficult in discovery-to-alpha work. The storyboard-based testing was directionally useful but could not fully validate workflow fit; participants were reacting to the concept of the service rather than experiencing it under real operational pressure. I would build in a stage of more detailed task-based testing with refined prototypes earlier, to catch workflow mismatches before the prioritisation workshop commits to a direction.

Thank you for reading

Research should change things. Every project in this portfolio did. I would welcome the conversation about how that could extend to your team.
